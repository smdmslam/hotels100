The first principle is:

> They will not come to DMW merely because the same expensive hotel can also be booked there.

Booking.com already handles availability, payment and confirmation extremely well. DMW must own the difficult decision that happens immediately before booking:

> “Given who I am, why I’m travelling, what I value and what I’m willing to spend, which hotel should I actually choose?”

The booking is then the commercially valuable conclusion of that decision.

For an $800–$1,000-plus traveller, “best price” rarely means cheapest. It means:

* I am not materially overpaying.
* The hotel will deliver what matters to me.
* I understand its compromises.
* There is not a substantially better option for this particular trip.
* My assistant or advisor can book it without creating a failure.

## The reasons principals come

### 1. To reduce luxury-hotel choice to a credible shortlist

A search for London may produce hundreds of nominally five-star hotels. The user does not want 600 results sorted by sponsored placement.

They want:

> “These are the five that genuinely fit you.”

DMW should reduce search rather than expand it.

### 2. To avoid expensive disappointment

At €1,200 per night, a mediocre decision is not merely a financial annoyance. It affects an important business trip, anniversary, family holiday or social occasion.

Common forms of luxury-hotel regret include:

* Famous hotel but tired rooms
* Magnificent lobby but small standard rooms
* Great for leisure but operationally poor for business
* Excellent location but severe street noise
* Beautiful resort requiring constant transportation
* “Five-star gym” that is effectively a small basement room
* Destination bar creating queues and noise
* Strong brand but inconsistent service
* High rate justified only during certain periods
* Renovation, construction or partially closed facilities
* Famous property whose best rooms are excellent but entry rooms are poor

DMW should explicitly identify these compromises.

That is more valuable than another flattering hotel description.

### 3. To understand who the hotel is actually for

The most useful hotel question is often not “Is it good?”

It is:

> “Is it good for me on this trip?”

Examples:

* Best for a solo business traveller
* Best for a couple wanting atmosphere
* Best for discreet family-office meetings
* Best for children without feeling like a family resort
* Best for walking access to a specific business district
* Best for nightlife
* Best for privacy
* Best for a first visit to the city
* Best for a repeat visitor who wants a neighbourhood experience
* Best for a principal travelling with security or staff
* Best for entertaining clients
* Best when the hotel itself is part of the trip

This is where Ask DMW becomes genuinely useful.

### 4. To know whether the rate makes sense

The high-end traveller may accept €1,500 per night but still wants to know whether €1,500 is intelligent.

DMW can answer:

* Is this rate normal for the hotel?
* Is it unusually expensive this week?
* When does the property enter a better-value window?
* Is a nearby higher-ranked hotel only €100 more?
* Is the standard room poor value relative to the first suite category?
* Is the hotel charging event-driven compression pricing?
* Does this property hold its rate because it is genuinely scarce?
* Which similar hotels are temporarily mispriced?

This is differentiated and commercially powerful.

A strong prompt might be:

> “I’m in Zurich for three nights, need a proper gym and quiet room, and would prefer to remain below €900 unless the upgrade is genuinely worthwhile.”

That exposes:

* Destination
* Dates
* Budget
* Trip type
* Amenity requirement
* Noise sensitivity
* Willingness to stretch
* Value philosophy

That is excellent intent data.

### 5. To compare unlike hotels intelligently

Booking sites compare prices and amenities. They are weaker at explaining the underlying choice:

> The Connaught versus Claridge’s versus The Berkeley is not merely a rate comparison.

DMW should explain:

* Atmosphere
* Clientele
* Service personality
* Location trade-offs
* Room quality
* Public-space intensity
* Privacy
* Brand position
* Business utility
* Emotional experience
* Price justification

A user should be able to select three hotels and receive:

> “Choose A if this matters; B if this matters; avoid C for this trip because…”

### 6. To obtain information that is difficult to find elsewhere

Examples:

* Which room categories to avoid
* Whether the entry room is genuinely acceptable
* Which wing or orientation is preferable
* Whether the hotel is noisy
* Whether the bar dominates the atmosphere
* How late breakfast is actually served
* Whether there are queues
* Whether room service is truly 24-hour
* Whether the gym is serious
* Whether the hotel handles late arrival well
* Whether a suite has a real working area
* Whether the neighbourhood works at night
* How long the transfer really feels
* Whether service is formal, warm, inconsistent or intrusive
* Whether the clientele is corporate, leisure, fashion, family or transient

This is where field reports and the Insider View earn their existence.

### 7. To discover hotels outside their habitual set

Affluent travellers often repeat the same brands because repetition reduces risk.

DMW can safely introduce alternatives:

> “You normally stay at the Four Seasons, but for this trip these two independent hotels better fit your location and room requirements.”

That is much more useful than generic inspiration.

### 8. To preserve personal hotel preferences

Over time, DMW should remember:

* Preferred room size
* Firm versus soft beds, if available
* Gym importance
* Breakfast habits
* Noise sensitivity
* Modern versus heritage preference
* Preferred neighbourhoods
* Business versus leisure pattern
* Need for parking
* Family composition
* Pet requirements
* Typical budget
* Willingness to stretch
* Brands previously liked or rejected
* Preference for privacy versus social atmosphere
* Need for a proper desk
* Importance of late checkout

Then Ask DMW becomes progressively better.

The user should eventually be able to say:

> “Find something in Paris for next week.”

And DMW already knows what “something” means.

### 9. To access a better booking proposition

Once DMW has helped select the hotel, the transaction can improve beyond a plain redirect:

* Compare direct and partner pricing
* Breakfast included
* Upgrade eligibility
* Hotel credit
* Early check-in
* Late checkout
* Flexible cancellation
* Better room category
* Special packages
* Preferred-partner benefits
* DMW-specific offer
* Human help for a complicated booking

At the high end, two rates that look equal may have meaningfully different total value.

DMW should eventually show:

> €1,150 direct
> €1,175 with breakfast, €100 credit and upgrade eligibility

The second may be the better booking.

### 10. To have one trusted source instead of ten browser tabs

The current luxury booking process often involves:

* Google
* Booking.com
* Hotel website
* Tripadvisor
* Instagram
* Google Maps
* Travel publications
* YouTube
* An advisor
* WhatsApp messages

DMW’s opportunity is to collapse that fragmented research into one decision environment.

# Why an executive assistant comes

The assistant’s job is different. The assistant is not primarily seeking inspiration.

They want to avoid presenting a bad choice to the principal.

### 1. Produce a defensible shortlist quickly

> “Three suitable Zurich hotels within 15 minutes of the meeting, proper gym, quiet rooms, flexible cancellation, below €1,000.”

The assistant does not want 70 search results. They want three acceptable recommendations with explanations.

### 2. Use stored principal preferences

The assistant should not repeatedly reconstruct:

* Mr. X dislikes fashionable noisy hotels.
* Mrs. X requires a spa.
* They prefer a suite with a separate living area.
* Breakfast must begin before 7:00.
* The principal will pay more for proximity.
* The traveller refuses certain brands.

A saved traveller profile is extremely useful.

### 3. Create something presentable

DMW can produce a clean decision brief:

| Hotel   |   Rate | Why it fits            | Compromise         | Booking terms      |
| ------- | -----: | ---------------------- | ------------------ | ------------------ |
| Hotel A |   €820 | Closest; excellent gym | Less atmospheric   | Flexible           |
| Hotel B |   €940 | Best overall service   | 18-minute transfer | Breakfast included |
| Hotel C | €1,080 | Best suite product     | Above budget       | Upgrade eligible   |

The assistant can email or WhatsApp the brief to the principal.

### 4. Record why a decision was made

This matters when several people are involved:

* Principal
* Spouse
* Assistant
* Travel advisor
* Family-office staff
* Corporate travel department

A saved shortlist creates an audit trail:

> “Claridge’s was selected because the Connaught was €600 more for the required dates and the traveller needed a larger working area.”

### 5. Avoid operational failures

The assistant needs:

* Cancellation deadline
* Payment requirements
* Check-in arrangements
* Airport transfer
* Connecting rooms
* Accessibility
* Parking
* Pet policy
* Late arrival
* Invoice requirements
* Room-category confirmation

DMW can become a structured verification layer rather than merely a recommendation site.

### 6. Reuse previous trips

> “Book something equivalent to the hotel he liked in Milan.”

That is a powerful use of preference and stay history.

# Why a luxury travel advisor comes

The advisor has another set of motivations.

### 1. Independent comparative intelligence

The advisor may know individual hotels extremely well but still benefit from:

* DMW scoring
* Pricing curves
* Competitive comparisons
* New-opening analysis
* Ownership/operator context
* Structured city-wide coverage

### 2. Faster research for unfamiliar destinations

No advisor has firsthand knowledge of every luxury hotel in every city.

DMW can become the advisor’s research layer.

### 3. A differentiated client presentation

The advisor can use a DMW-powered shortlist rather than forwarding hotel links.

### 4. Better conversion

A client who understands why a hotel fits is more likely to approve the booking quickly.

### 5. Commercial participation

Eventually an advisor might:

* Book through DMW infrastructure
* Retain or share commission
* Use DMW as a white-label research layer
* Pay for professional access
* Add human recommendations to an AI-generated shortlist

This potentially creates a B2B2C distribution channel.

# Why family offices and private clubs come

They may want:

* A controlled list of approved hotels
* Preferred properties by destination
* Security and privacy considerations
* Known room and suite configurations
* Principal-specific preferences
* Centralized booking history
* Consistent recommendations across staff
* Direct hotel relationships
* Negotiated benefits
* Reliable handling of special requests

This is closer to hotel procurement and member service than travel media.

# The strongest reasons to build first

There are dozens of possible reasons, but the MVP should concentrate on five:

## 1. Better shortlist

> Find the right three hotels, not every available hotel.

## 2. Better fit

> Explain which hotel matches this traveller and trip.

## 3. Better value judgment

> Determine whether the current rate is justified and what alternatives exist.

## 4. Better practical verification

> Gym, parking, breakfast, room service, noise, workability, policies and room realities.

## 5. Better handoff to booking

> Take the chosen hotel directly into a trackable, benefit-aware booking path.

Everything else can follow.

# The questions Ask DMW should progressively elicit

It should not begin with a 20-field form. It should begin conversationally and ask only what materially changes the answer.

### Core trip intent

* Destination
* Dates
* Number of nights
* Number of travellers
* Business, leisure or mixed
* Target nightly budget
* Maximum stretch budget

### Hotel fit

* Preferred neighbourhood or required destination
* Classic, contemporary or no preference
* Large hotel versus intimate
* Social atmosphere versus privacy
* Brand preference
* Minimum room or suite requirement

### Practical requirements

* Gym
* Pool/spa
* Parking
* Family rooms
* Connecting rooms
* Pet friendly
* Accessibility
* Early breakfast
* 24-hour room service
* Proper work desk
* Quiet room
* Airport transfer

### Decision philosophy

* Best overall
* Best value
* Most prestigious
* Most convenient
* Most private
* Best service
* Most distinctive
* Safest choice
* Something new

The key is that every answer should visibly improve the shortlist. Otherwise the user will perceive the questions as friction.

# The central product promise

I would articulate it internally as:

> **DMW helps demanding travellers and their agents choose the right high-end hotel, understand the rate and book with confidence.**

That is a real reason to return.

Booking.com answers:

> What is available?

DMW should answer:

> Which one should you choose—and why, given this particular trip?

If DMW owns that answer, the booking click, the subscription, the hotel influence and the acquisition value all follow from the same underlying behavior.
