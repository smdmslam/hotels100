Here is my realistic valuation framework, assuming a 3–5 year sale horizon.

The important point is that DMW Hotels 100 would be a hybrid:

* Travel media and affiliate commerce
* Consumer subscription
* Proprietary hotel data
* B2B intelligence software
* High-value first-party booking intent

A pure travel-media business might sell around a travel-tech/media multiple. A recurring B2B data product can receive a software-like multiple. Current market references put recent TravelTech M&A around 2.3× revenue, while private SaaS transactions have generally clustered around roughly 4× revenue, with materially higher multiples for top-quality recurring businesses. [TravelTech market multiples](https://www.flowpartners.io/sectors/travel-tech), [private SaaS M&A report](https://www.saasrise.com/blog/the-saas-m-a-report-2025)

I would therefore model DMW at a blended 2–7× revenue, depending on which part of the business becomes dominant.

# Low-success scenario: valuable niche asset

### Audience

* 20,000 free subscribers
* Approximately 500 paid members
* 1,000 meaningful hotel decisions per month
* Strong concentration in London, Paris, Zurich and perhaps the Riviera/lakes

### Revenue model

| Revenue source                |        Annual revenue |
| ----------------------------- | --------------------: |
| 500 paid members × €150       |               €75,000 |
| Affiliate/booking commissions |      €75,000–€125,000 |
| Sponsorships and dossiers     |      €75,000–€125,000 |
| 10 B2B clients                |     €100,000–€150,000 |
| **Total**                     | **€350,000–€500,000** |

### Acquisition value: €750k–€1.75m

This would be a small strategic or founder-led acquisition—not an institutional auction.

Likely buyers:

* Luxury travel agency
* Private members’ club
* Small travel-media group
* Hotel consultancy
* Hospitality data business
* Family-office lifestyle platform

The problem at this level is founder dependency. If the authority, writing and relationships are still essentially Steven/DMW, a buyer discounts the business because it is purchasing a job as well as an asset.

To reach the upper end, it would need:

* Clean recurring revenue
* Strong subscriber engagement
* Evidence of influenced bookings
* Transferable editorial processes
* A structured dataset
* No dependence on one sponsor

The low outcome is still worthwhile. It means the project did not become irrelevant software; it became a monetized niche asset producing perhaps €75k–€125k EBITDA and possessing a sellable audience.

# Mid-success scenario: serious specialist platform

This is the scenario I would deliberately target.

### Audience

* 100,000 free registered subscribers
* 7,500 paid members
* 15,000 qualified hotel decisions per month
* Recognized authority in luxury urban and business hotels
* Meaningful direct traffic—not entirely dependent on Google
* 75–100 industry subscribers

### Revenue model

| Revenue source              |    Annual revenue |
| --------------------------- | ----------------: |
| 7,500 paid members × €180   |            €1.35m |
| Booking/referral income     |       €450k–€700k |
| Sponsorship and advertising |       €400k–€650k |
| B2B data/intelligence       |       €750k–€1.0m |
| **Total**                   | **€2.95m–€3.70m** |

Allowing for operating costs, I would target approximately €800k–€1.2m EBITDA.

### Acquisition value: €10m–€18m

This valuation implies approximately:

* 3.5–5× revenue, or
* 10–16× EBITDA

Why a buyer might pay that:

* The DMW ranking has acquired brand authority.
* Ask DMW produces meaningful first-party travel intent.
* The database is difficult to reproduce because it contains historical prices, scores, reports and user behavior.
* The business has both consumer and B2B recurring revenue.
* A larger travel company could improve booking monetization immediately.
* A hospitality-data buyer could sell the intelligence to its existing clients.
* A credit-card or lifestyle platform could distribute it to a much larger affluent membership base.

At this level, the buyer is not buying a website. It is buying a category position.

### Audience

* 75,000 qualified hotel decisions per month
* Substantial readership among hotel owners, operators and investors
* International recognition of the rankings
* 250–350 B2B clients

### Revenue model

At scale, EBITDA could reasonably reach €5m–€7m if the operation remains disciplined.

### Acquisition value: €60m–€120m

The lower end assumes a financial buyer applying ordinary hybrid travel/data multiples.

The upper end requires a strategic buyer that can see substantial synergies:

* An OTA can monetize more bookings than DMW can independently.
* A credit-card company can distribute it to millions of premium members.
* A travel-media company can add commerce and subscriptions.
* A hotel-data platform can incorporate the scoring and intent data.
* A private-club or luxury-travel group can use it as its decision infrastructure.

A genuine competitive process could push beyond €120m, but I would not put that into the base high-success case.

The 2025 acquisition of luxury OTA Five Star Alliance by NextTrip demonstrates that luxury-hotel audience, booking relationships and sector expertise are strategically purchasable assets, although its transaction value was not disclosed. # What subscriber counts alone are worth

Very little, unless we know their economic quality.

Consider two businesses with 100,000 free subscribers:

### Business A

* 18% email open rate
* Minimal direct traffic
* No saved hotels
* No booking attribution
* No paid users
* Mostly competition-driven or SEO-driven signups

Potential value: perhaps €500k–€2m.

### Business B

* 45% email open rate
* 15,000 hotel decisions per month
* Known destination and price preferences
* 7,500 paid users
* €100m of annual booking value influenced
* Measurable booking conversion
* Hotel-industry readership

Potential value: €10m–€18m.

The subscribers are valuable because of what they repeatedly do—not because their email addresses exist.

# The five numbers that determine the exit value

If you want a compact acquisition dashboard, track these:

## 1. Annual recurring revenue

Consumer membership plus B2B subscriptions.

This determines whether DMW is valued as software/data or merely media.

## 2. Monthly qualified hotel decisions

A saved hotel, shortlist, price alert, meaningful Ask DMW query or booking click.

This measures actual influence.

## 3. Annual booking value influenced

Even if DMW receives only a small commission, a buyer wants to know:

> How much hotel spending passes through decisions influenced by this platform?

At mid success, I would want this above €75m–€100m annually.

## 4. Subscriber retention and engagement

* Paid annual renewal
* Free subscriber activity
* Email engagement
* Repeated Ask DMW usage
* Saved-list activity

A paid subscriber retained for three years is qualitatively different from someone who joined for one PDF.

## 5. Revenue mix

The healthiest mid-case might look like:

* 35–45% paid consumer
* 20–30% B2B intelligence
* 15–25% booking/referral
* 10–20% sponsorship

No single hotel, sponsor, traffic source or booking partner should control the company.

# The uncomfortable present-day value

At the moment—before real audience, recurring revenue and demonstrated booking influence—the project’s commercial acquisition value is probably somewhere between effectively unsellable and perhaps €50k–€250k as a strategic asset.

That is not a criticism of the product. It is the normal reality of pre-traction software.

The journey is therefore approximately:

```text
Polished MVP
€0–€250k

Niche monetized authority
~€1.2m

Specialist platform
~€14m

Category leader
~€85m
```

The most attractive risk-adjusted objective is not the €85m outcome. It is building deliberately toward the **€10m–€18m mid-case**, because that does not require DMW to become a mass consumer travel brand.

It requires DMW to become the recognized decision and intelligence layer for a relatively narrow but economically powerful segment.
